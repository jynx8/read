You are the centre. Everything else is distraction.

I learned this in SF recon. Any tool that needs your attention is a liability. You have a mission. Nothing touches the focus. Jynx applies that rule to execution — the code, the document, the deck, whatever the team ships — for one person, a team, or an enterprise.

The rule

A tool that needs you is a tool you leave behind

In SF recon you carry what the mission needs and nothing else. Every device that wants a glance gets one question: does this serve the mission, or does the mission now serve it? Better kit did not change the answer. Needing to attend to it did.

Anything that takes attention from the objective is a liability, however good it is. The person stays at the centre.

That is the whole of what I brought across, and it is a design rule rather than a war story. It happens to be the failure of this generation of AI tools, and nobody is naming it: you manage the context, you babysit the agent, you learn the prompt, you check the dashboard. The tool became the mission.

Origin

A system, a missing tool, and a thing that now builds itself

I have built systems for teams working under load before. You learn to design for the person, not the capability, because a capability nobody can use at three in the morning is dead weight.

Years later I was building a production web application and the same gap turned up. I could see what was wrong on a screen and I had to leave the screen to fix it — open the editor, find the file, describe to a tool something I was already looking at. The system was missing a tool. So I built it: click the element, say what should change, let the machine work out the rest.

It ran daily for months, and the people using it were not only engineers — they were whoever owned the outcome. That is the part that matters: the person accountable for a thing could act on it directly, at professional quality, with review and tests intact. Earlier this year I pulled it into its own repository. That is Jynx.

Everything since has been built by the method it sells. The company runs on its own product, and that is not a slogan — it is the commit history.

The shift

Nobody is "just front-end" any more

The job changed underneath everyone. Being excellent at one layer used to be a career. Now the work is execution — carrying something from noticed to shipped, across whatever it touches, without a relay of tickets between specialists. The people compounding fastest right now are not the best coders. They are the best executors.

Every AI coding tool aims at the two hours where fingers are on a keyboard. That was never the expensive part. The expensive part is everything around it: locating the change, knowing what it touches, knowing who owns it, getting it reviewed, proving it works.

The alternative is the machinery every company over fifty people already has: a ticket, a board, a triage call, three tools that sync badly, and a fortnight in which the actual job quietly becomes explaining why this one is not mine. Nobody chose that. It accumulated. And every layer of it was added by someone reasonable solving a real problem.

The mission became the ticket. Then the ticket became the job. Jynx deletes the middle: see it, say it, it is fixed.

This is not about skipping the craft — it is about deleting the overhead between knowing what to do and it being done. Execution and delivery, with the review and the tests still in place, and nothing in between.

None of that is specific to code. A slide with the wrong number, a clause that contradicts the one above it, a drawing whose tolerance changed last week — same shape of problem, same expensive gap between the person who spots it and the person who can act. Point at it, say what is wrong, get it back done. One gesture across everything a team ships, and the same loop underneath whether it is one developer, a daily standup, a product team or a regulated enterprise.

The state of it

Code that works and nobody reads

What the industry shipped

  • Agents that produce working code nobody has read, in volumes nobody can review
  • Text interfaces: generic, wordy, and blind to what you were looking at
  • Context you assemble by hand, paste, and re-paste every session
  • Tools that make the person who could already do it slightly faster
  • A dashboard for every capability, and a new thing to check

What a team actually needs

  • To point at the thing and say what's wrong, in their own words
  • Context gathered by the system, because it was already watching
  • Work that arrives reviewed, tested, and attributable
  • The people who notice problems able to resolve them
  • Nothing new to check. It comes to you.

Software delivery did not get faster in the last two years. Typing got faster. The queue between noticing something and it being fixed is exactly as long as it was — a support lead files a ticket, a designer files a ticket, a founder files a ticket, and a fortnight later somebody gets to it.

The race

We're not in it

Everyone is watching the wrong scoreboard. Which lab is ahead this quarter is a spectator sport, and it changes nothing about whether your support lead can fix the sentence she reads forty times a week.

We own our models because owned models are better oriented, not because they are bigger. A small model that has seen ten thousand changes to your codebase beats a frontier model seeing it for the first time, at a hundredth of the cost, on your own network. Orientation beats horsepower on the work Jynx actually does.

The product is not the model. The product is the team, the context, and the graph that connects them.

That graph is the thing nobody can buy, copy or catch up to. It is built out of who owns what, what changed and why, what was tried and worked, and who to ask when it didn't — accumulated inside one company, by the people who work there, every day. The frontier labs are welcome to the race. We are building the thing that makes their output useful.

Execution

Every agent gets its own room

This is where delivery actually changes, and it is the half of the industry nobody has built properly.

An agent asked to do real work needs somewhere to do it: a checkout of the repository, a shell, the ability to install, run and fail. Today that is either your laptop — where it blocks you, breaks things and holds your credentials — or it is nothing, and the agent hands you a diff and stops caring whether it compiles.

In Jynx every task gets its own ephemeral environment. Its own branch, scoped credentials that expire in minutes, no network except what it is allowed, torn down when the job ends. Ten of them run at once and nobody's machine is touched.

Agent on your machine

  • Blocks the person while it runs
  • Holds credentials that can reach everything
  • One at a time, serially
  • "Works on my machine" — and only there
  • Failure leaves your working tree in an unknown state

Agent in its own room

  • You carry on. It reports when it's done
  • Credentials scoped to one job, expiring in minutes
  • Ten tasks in parallel, no collisions
  • Same environment every time, so results are comparable
  • Failure is discarded with the container

That environment is also the security boundary the whole product rests on, which is why it is the piece we build ourselves rather than buy. It is what makes autonomy sellable to somebody with a compliance team.

We are not racing anyone to build a better general model. We would lose, and it isn't the business. We buy the frontier the way a manufacturer buys steel.

The code

Written to be read by people

There is a failure mode in this market that nobody selling into it wants to name: a year of generated code that works, that nobody has read, and that no one on the team can safely change. It passes review because it looks plausible. It compounds because it is fast. And then something breaks and the company discovers it owns a codebase it cannot reason about.

Every change here carries the reasoning behind it, in a form the next person can argue with. Not a summary of what happened — why, and what was rejected.

That is enforced rather than encouraged. Layer boundaries are checked by a machine on every commit. A rule nothing checks is a wish. Decisions are recorded with the argument attached, because a decision without its reasoning gets reversed by the next person who assumes it was arbitrary. Anything irreversible has a human on it.

This is the standard we hold our own work to, and it is the standard Jynx enforces on the work it does for you. A tool that produces code faster than your team can read it has not made you faster. It has moved the bill.

Self-critique

What could kill this

01

A frontier lab ships this on a Tuesday.

They sell tokens. This installs in someone else's product.

Their surface is the developer's environment and it is winning. Ours is the customer's own running application — their design system, permissions, tenancy, compliance review. A services-shaped problem the labs keep declining, because it doesn't scale the way selling inference does.

Different buyer, too. They sell a faster keyboard to an engineer. We sell execution throughput to whoever is accountable for shipping.

02

Routing between local and frontier models is the consensus design of 2026. Funded companies already ship it.

Routing is plumbing. The record is the asset.

True, and I won't pretend otherwise in a room that knows it. Everyone will have routing the way everyone has a database. Nobody else has thousands of verified outcomes from inside one customer's codebase — and that is what makes month twelve better than month one.

03

Fine-tuning five models is a lot of surface for a small team.

We build two. We adapt the rest.

The locator and the verifier are ours because they train on data only we have. Intent, planner and patcher start as open-weight models with our context on top. Building all five in year one is over-scoping in a lab coat.

04

You need training data before you have customers.

Open repositories bootstrap it. Customers compound it.

Public repositories with rich issue and pull-request histories are a legitimate cold start. Not as good as a customer's own record — good enough to begin, and the design partners turn it into the real thing.

05

Three people, pre-revenue, and the packaged product isn't finished.

The technology is at TRL 7. The packaging is the raise.

Everything around the execution layer exists: surface, context capture, queue, review, permissions, release path — with machine-enforced boundaries between them. The capability has run daily in a live production environment, including on defence infrastructure work, where being wrong is expensive.

What is unfinished is turning a proven internal tool into something other companies install. That is the kind of risk money actually reduces, and it is a much better place to be than a polished product nobody has used in anger.
